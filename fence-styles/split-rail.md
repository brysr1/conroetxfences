faqs:
  - q: "Will a split rail fence hold my dog?"
    a: "Not by itself. We attach welded wire or no-climb mesh to the inside of the rails, which keeps dogs in while the fence keeps its open look."
  - q: "Two rails or three?"
    a: "Two rails is the lighter, more decorative look. Three rails stands taller and does a better job with horses or as a frame for wire mesh."
  - q: "How long does a wood rail fence last?"
    a: "It depends on the wood, ground contact, and upkeep. Posts in wet ground fail first. A sealer on the rails and posts set for the soil add years, and vinyl rail skips the sealing altogether."

      <h2>Rails, posts, and open space</h2>
      <p>Split rail fencing uses rough-split rails that slide into holes cut through the posts, two or three rails high. Post-and-rail, sometimes called board fence, nails sawn boards onto square posts for a tidier take on the same idea.</p>
      <p>Both usually stand between three and four-plus feet, mark a boundary, and leave the view open. Along a county road, at the head of a gravel drive, or across a front pasture under the pines, they look like they belong.</p>

    <div class="lc-head">
      <p class="lc-kicker">Local conditions</p>
      <h2>Rail fences in humid, stormy country</h2>
    </div>
    <ul class="lc-checks">
      <li><strong>Moisture.</strong> Posts rot first where the ground stays wet. We set them for the soil, and a sealer on the wood slows decay.</li>
      <li><strong>Sun.</strong> Untreated rails gray quickly. Some owners like that weathered look; others stain to keep the color.</li>
      <li><strong>Storms.</strong> Limbs knock rails out of their posts. Most come back with a single replacement rail.</li>
      <li><strong>Long runs.</strong> Solid end and corner posts keep the line straight, after a Texas 811 locate.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>
