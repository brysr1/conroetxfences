faqs:
  - q: "What fence is safest for horses?"
    a: "No-climb woven mesh, usually with a board or pipe top rail so horses can see it, is the common choice. Barbed wire is not recommended for horses."
  - q: "Why do corners and braces matter so much?"
    a: "A wire fence is only as tight as its ends. H-braces and corner assemblies hold the tension, and when they are built right the wire stays tight for years instead of sagging after the first wet season."
  - q: "Can you fence a wooded lot without clearing it?"
    a: "Usually we clear a narrow path for the line and work around larger trees. We walk it with you first and talk through what needs to come out."
  - q: "My old fence doesn't seem to follow the property line. What then?"
    a: "On older and rural tracts, fence lines often don't match the deeded boundary. If there is any doubt, a survey before we build is the safest step, and we stake the new line from it."
  - q: "How often should a ranch fence be checked?"
    a: "Walk it after every big storm and at least once or twice a year. Look for limbs on the wire, loose staples or clips, and posts that have moved."

      <h2>Cost versus looks versus what it holds</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="lc-grid lc-grid-2">
      <div class="lc-card lc-card--flat"><div class="lc-card-body">
        <h3>Wire fences</h3>
        <p><strong>Good:</strong> lowest cost per foot, fast to build on long lines, and easy to splice after a limb comes down.</p>
        <p><strong>Watch:</strong> depends on well-built braces, needs re-tensioning over time, and offers little visual presence.</p>
      </div></div>
      <div class="lc-card lc-card--flat"><div class="lc-card-body">
        <h3>Board, rail &amp; pipe</h3>
        <p><strong>Good:</strong> highly visible to livestock, handsome along a road, and pipe is close to permanent.</p>
        <p><strong>Watch:</strong> costs several times more per foot, and wood needs sealing while steel needs paint.</p>
      </div></div>
    </div>
    <p class="lc-note lc-mt">A common plan: rail or pipe along the frontage and around the house, field wire or no-climb across the back acreage.</p>

    <div class="lc-head">
      <p class="lc-kicker">Local conditions</p>
      <h2>Keeping a ranch fence tight in East Texas</h2>
    </div>
    <ul class="lc-checks">
      <li><strong>Humidity.</strong> Heavier galvanizing on the wire buys years of extra life in damp air and wet grass.</li>
      <li><strong>Wet clay and loose sand.</strong> Brace assemblies matter even more in low, soggy spots and sandy ridges. Every job starts with a Texas 811 locate.</li>
      <li><strong>Pine and hardwood limbs.</strong> Wooded tracts lose branches in every storm. A well-braced line is easier to clear and re-tighten.</li>
      <li><strong>Heat and drought.</strong> Dry spells shrink the ground and loosen posts. A walk-through each season catches it early.</li>
      <li><strong>Creeks and draws.</strong> Seasonal creeks need water gaps or crossings that survive high water.</li>
      <li><strong>Wood sealing, pipe paint.</strong> Board fences benefit from a sealer; painted pipe needs touch-ups where it chips.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>
