  - q: "Is black coated chain link worth the extra cost?"
    a: "For anything visible from the house or street, most people think so. The vinyl coating adds rust protection and blends into trees and lawn far better than bright galvanized wire."
  - q: "Can I get privacy with chain link?"
    a: "Some. Slats woven through the mesh block most of the view and cost less than a wood fence. They do add wind load, so we set posts with that in mind."
  - q: "Will chain link keep my dog in?"
    a: "It works well for most medium and large dogs. For diggers we add a bottom tension wire or bury the base, and for climbers we talk about height. Very small dogs may need a smaller mesh."
  - q: "Do HOAs allow chain link?"
    a: "Some limit it, especially where it faces a street. We check the current rules for your property before quoting and suggest a coated or alternative option if bare galvanized isn't allowed."

      <h2>Built to work, not to be looked at</h2>
      <p>Chain link is woven steel wire stretched between round posts and held tight by a top rail and tension bands. It won&rsquo;t hide anything, but it keeps dogs, kids, and equipment where they belong, and it costs less per foot than any other fence we build for most jobs.</p>
      <p>Bright galvanized wire is the economy version. Black or green vinyl-coated mesh, with matching posts, fades into a treeline and looks far more finished from the porch. Heavier gauges and taller heights are used for commercial lots and storage yards.</p>

    <div class="lc-head">
      <p class="lc-kicker">Local conditions</p>
      <h2>Chain link care in East Texas weather</h2>
    </div>
    <ul class="lc-checks">
      <li><strong>Humid air.</strong> Galvanizing slows rust; a vinyl coating adds another layer. Cut ends and scratched fittings are where rust shows first.</li>
      <li><strong>Sun.</strong> Coated mesh is made for UV exposure and holds its color.</li>
      <li><strong>Clay and sand.</strong> Corner, end, and gate posts carry the tension for the whole run, so they get the deepest footings. Texas 811 marks utility lines before we dig.</li>
      <li><strong>Storms.</strong> Wind passes through. Falling limbs can kink a top rail, which is a quick, low-cost fix.</li>
      <li><strong>Vines and brush.</strong> Pull them off the fabric each season so they don&rsquo;t stretch it.</li>
      <li><strong>Gates.</strong> If a gate starts to sag or drag, the hinges and latch usually just need adjusting.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>
