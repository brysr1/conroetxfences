faqs:
  - q: "Should I pick cedar or pressure-treated pine?"
    a: "Cedar costs more up front, has a warmer color, and resists rot and bugs on its own. Treated pine is the budget pick and takes stain well. Many Conroe fences use pine posts or steel posts with cedar pickets, and we price both so you can compare."
  - q: "How soon should a new wood fence be stained?"
    a: "Give new boards a few weeks to dry out, then seal them within the first few months. After that, plan on a fresh coat every few years, sooner on the sides that face the afternoon sun or a sprinkler head."
  - q: "Do steel posts make sense with a wood fence?"
    a: "Often, yes. Galvanized steel posts don't rot at the ground line, which is where most wood fences fail around here. The wood pickets and rails still give you the look of a wood fence."
  - q: "Can my HOA tell me what stain color to use?"
    a: "Many community associations review fence color along with height and style. We don't guess at those rules. We check the current guidelines for your lot and include the stain details in your submittal."

      <p class="lc-kicker">Why wood</p>
      <h2>Warm, familiar, and easy to fix</h2>
      <p>Wood is still the default choice behind most houses in Montgomery County. It blocks the view, softens a yard full of new construction, and can be stained to sit quietly next to brick or siding.</p>
      <p>Two species do most of the work. Western red cedar has a reddish tone and natural oils that slow rot and insects. Pressure-treated pine is less expensive, a little plainer, and holds stain well. If a picket cracks or a mower clips a board, the repair is a single board and a few nails.</p>
      <p>How long a wood fence lasts here comes down to three things: posts set for the soil, boards kept off the ground, and a sealer that goes on early and gets renewed.</p>

        <ul class="lc-checks lc-checks--one">
          <li><strong>Regular sealing.</strong> Skip it and boards gray, cup, and split faster in our sun.</li>
          <li><strong>Moisture at the base.</strong> Wet clay and sprinkler spray are hard on the bottom of the fence.</li>
          <li><strong>Wind load.</strong> A solid six-foot run acts like a sail during storms, so posts matter.</li>
          <li><strong>Movement.</strong> Boards shrink and swell with the seasons, which is why style choice counts.</li>

    <div class="lc-head">
      <p class="lc-kicker">Local conditions</p>
      <h2>Keeping wood in shape through East Texas weather</h2>
    </div>
    <ul class="lc-checks">
      <li><strong>Humidity and rain.</strong> Around here, water does more damage to wood fences than anything else. A rot board keeps pickets out of wet grass, and a penetrating sealer keeps water out of the grain.</li>
      <li><strong>Summer sun.</strong> UV grays bare wood within a season or two. A stain with UV blockers slows it, and south- and west-facing runs usually need recoating first.</li>
      <li><strong>Clay that swells and shrinks.</strong> Much of the ground around Conroe moves with wet and dry spells. We set posts deep enough for the lot and request Texas 811 locates before digging.</li>
      <li><strong>Storms and falling limbs.</strong> Wooded lots lose branches in every big blow. Well-set posts keep a damaged section from pulling the whole line over, and a broken bay is a quick repair.</li>
      <li><strong>Sprinklers and beds.</strong> Aim heads away from the boards and keep mulch and soil below the bottom rail.</li>
      <li><strong>A yearly look.</strong> Walk the line each spring for loose pickets, popped nails, and a gate that has started to drag.</li>
    </ul>
