<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> A well-built wood fence here often lasts somewhere in the 10 to 18 year range, with cedar at the longer end and treated pine at the shorter end. Vinyl, aluminum, coated steel, and composite often go 20 years or more. On wooded, shaded, or wet lots, how the posts were set and how the fence is kept up can move those numbers a lot in either direction.</p>
</div>

<h2>What wears fences out early around here</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Posts set short or in a flooded hole.</strong> Our sandy topsoil sits over slow-draining clay, so water can collect in a fresh hole. Concrete poured into standing water cures weak.</li>
  <li><strong>Pine straw and mulch against the boards.</strong> On wooded lots in Conroe and Willis, debris piles up along the base and holds water for days after a rain.</li>
  <li><strong>Sprinklers hitting the same pickets.</strong> Daily spray rots the bottom of a wood fence years ahead of the rest.</li>
  <li><strong>Unsealed wood.</strong> Bare boards gray, check, and split faster in our sun and humidity.</li>
  <li><strong>Limbs and vines.</strong> A fence under mature pines takes hits in every storm, and vines slowly pull mesh and boards out of line.</li>
  <li><strong>Light builder posts.</strong> Many newer subdivision fences went in on a tight budget, and some start leaning within a few years.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What adds years</h2>
<p>Most of the life of a wood fence is decided on install day. A rot board keeps pickets off wet ground, posts sunk 24 inches or more and backed by 80 pounds of concrete hold their line, and steel posts behind the pickets take rot out of the picture. After that, a sealer in the first few months and a fresh coat every few years does the most good. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a> crew handles that.</p>
<p>Keep the base clear, aim sprinkler heads away, and trim limbs that hang over the line.
