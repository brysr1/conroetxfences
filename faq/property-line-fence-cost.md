<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Nobody has to chip in by default. Texas has no statute that splits fence costs between adjoining owners, so the person who wants the fence pays unless the neighbors agree to share it or the deed restrictions say otherwise. Where state law does step in is removing a fence the two of you own together.</p>
</div>

<h2>The default: whoever builds it pays</h2>
<p>Texas A&amp;M AgriLife's fence-law guide, <a href="https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/files/2022/07/Second-Edition-Five-Strands.pdf" rel="noopener"><em>Five Strands</em></a>, puts it plainly: a landowner has no legal duty to help pay for or maintain a fence a neighbor builds, unless they agreed to. If the neighbor doesn't contribute, the fence belongs to the owner who paid for it. The guide adds that this holds even after a storm takes a fence down. Nobody owes a share of the rebuild.</p>
<p>That surprises people most when a builder fence was put up for both lots at once and both owners assumed the replacement would be split too. Plenty of neighbors do split the cost, since they usually want the same fence. That's a private deal, and it should be written down.</p>

<h2>How it plays out on different lots</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Small subdivision lots.</strong> In Cielo, lots run 40 to 50 feet wide, and Mavera's are around a seventh of an acre. Nearly every run touches a neighbor, so a replacement is almost always a shared conversation, and the HOA's rules come into it too.</li>
  <li><strong>Older neighborhoods without an HOA.</strong> In places like Lake Chateau Woods, no committee sets the style, so the two owners have to agree on height, material, and which face goes where.</li>
  <li><strong>Acreage with livestock next door.</strong> Around Willis, Huntsville, and Cleveland, the neighbor who keeps cattle or horses may want a tighter fence than you need, which is a good reason for them to pay a bigger share.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Removing a shared fence takes consent or notice</h2>
<p>Texas Agriculture Code <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.143.htm#143.121" rel="noopener">sections 143.121 through 143.123</a> limit tearing out a dividing fence two owners own jointly, or a fence attached to someone else's fence. One owner can't remove a jointly owned fence without the other's consent. Where one fence is tied into another, removal takes consent or six months' notice, as AgriLife's <a href="https://agecoext.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2019/06/Overview-of-Texas-Fencing-Laws.pdf" rel="noopener">overview of Texas fencing laws</a> explains. It comes up when one neighbor wants a new style and the other likes the old one. Before replacing a fence you share, get the neighbor's okay in writing.</p>

<h2>Livestock and stock laws</h2>
<p>Texas starts out as open range, but local stock laws, adopted by county voters, close the range for the animals they name. <em>Five Strands</em> notes there's no reliable statewide list, that stock laws can cover all or part of a county and specific species, and that the county clerk can search the election records. Where a stock law applies to an animal, keeping it in falls on its owner. On acreage near Huntsville, Willis, or Cleveland, check which animals your county's law covers before you decide who needs the fence most.</p>

<h2>Put the deal on paper</h2>
<p><em>Five Strands</em> says written agreements to share a fence are binding, and it suggests recording them in the county deed records. A short agreement should cover:</p>
<ol>
  <li>Where the fence sits, tied to the survey or the corner pins</li>
  <li>Material, height, and which side faces each yard</li>
  <li>Each owner's share, including tear-out of the old fence and any gates</li>
  <li>Who handles staining, repairs, and storm damage later</li>
  <li>What happens if one of the houses sells</li>
</ol>

Typical per-foot ranges are on the <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Old fences that wander</h2>
<p>A fence that stood in the wrong spot for years doesn't automatically move the boundary. Adverse possession in Texas takes years of open, continuous use, generally about 10, and <em>Five Strands</em> notes that a fence put up just to hold livestock usually isn't enough on its own to claim land. Even so, an off-line fence on acreage is worth correcting when you rebuild, and a <a href="/faq/property-survey/">survey</a> shows where the new one belongs.</p>
