<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Nobody has to chip in by default. Texas has no statute that splits fence costs between adjoining owners, so the person who wants the fence pays unless the neighbors agree to share it or the deed restrictions say otherwise. Where state law does step in is removing a fence the two of you own together.</p>
</div>

<h2>The default: whoever builds it pays</h2>
<p>On a typical lot in Conroe, Willis, or Huntsville, a fence on your side of the line belongs to you. You pay for it, you pick it within HOA and city rules, and you keep it up. A neighbor who enjoys it doesn't owe you a share. That catches people off guard when the old builder fence was shared and both owners assumed the replacement would be too.</p>
<p>Splitting the cost happens all the time anyway, since two households usually want the same thing. That split is a private agreement between you, and the cleanest version is written down.</p>

<h2>Removing a shared fence takes consent or notice</h2>
<p>Texas Agriculture Code sections 143.121 through 143.123 cover a dividing fence that two owners own jointly, or a fence attached to someone else's fence. Put simply, one owner can't take it down without the other's consent or six months' written notice. It comes up when one neighbor wants to tear out a shared line for a new style and the other doesn't. Before replacing a fence you share, get the neighbor's okay in writing.</p>
<p>The same chapter deals with livestock. Montgomery County has had a county-wide cattle stock law since a 1956 election, and Walker, Liberty, and Polk counties are reported to have adopted stock laws in the 1950s too. Under a stock law, animals aren't supposed to roam at large, so keeping them in falls to the livestock owner. Stock laws can cover only part of a county, so on acreage near Huntsville or Cleveland, confirm with the County Clerk.</p>

<h2>Put the deal on paper</h2>
<p>A short written agreement signed before the fence goes up heads off most disputes. It should cover:</p>
<ol>
  <li>Where the fence sits, tied to the survey or the corner pins</li>
  <li>Material, height, and which side faces each yard</li>
  <li>How the cost splits, including tear-out of the old fence and any gates</li>
  <li>Who handles staining, repairs, and storm damage later</li>
  <li>What happens if one of the houses sells</li>
</ol>
<p>If you are splitting the cost, ask us to quote the shared stretch on its own to make the split simple. If your HOA reviews fences, get that approval before either of you commits money to a design. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page explains that side.</p>

<h2>Old fences and adverse possession</h2>
<p>A fence that stood in the wrong spot for years doesn't automatically move the boundary. Adverse possession claims in Texas carry long time requirements (one common path is 10 years under Chapter 16 of the Civil Practice and Remedies Code), and courts have treated a casual fence, like one strung for cattle or one that dodges a creek, as not enough on its own to claim land. Even so, an off-line fence on acreage is worth correcting when you rebuild. A <a href="/faq/property-survey/">survey</a> shows where the new one belongs.</p>
