<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Fences you can see through, like aluminum, ornamental steel, chain link, and split rail, let wind pass and rarely blow over. If you need privacy, shadowbox or other semi-privacy styles carry less load than a solid wall of boards. For any style, the depth and setting of the posts decide what survives a big storm.</p>
</div>

<h2>What our storms have actually done</h2>
<p>Inland Montgomery and Walker counties don't get the coastal surge, but they get the wind and the falling trees. The National Weather Service's <a href="https://www.weather.gov/hgx/hurricanes_climatology_2000s" rel="noopener">2000s hurricane summary</a> says Hurricane Rita in 2005 brought gusts near hurricane force to both counties, with damage mainly to fences, roofs, and mobile homes. Three years later, Ike killed one person in Montgomery County and one in Walker County when trees fell onto occupied houses.</p>
<p>More recently, the NWS's <a href="https://www.weather.gov/hgx/Beryl2024" rel="noopener">unofficial Beryl gust reports</a> for July 8, 2024 list 69 mph at the Conroe ASOS station, 59 mph at Huntsville, and 50 mph at Coldspring near Lake Livingston. Gusts like that find any post that was set short or sits in loose, wet sand.</p>

<h2>How wind loads a fence</h2>
<p>A solid 6-foot privacy fence works like a sail. Every square foot of board pushes on the rails, the rails push on the posts, and each post levers against the soil at its base. The ground decides how much of that the post can take.</p>
<p>Most Conroe-area lots have sandy topsoil over clay that drains slowly, which the <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">USDA's Conroe soil series</a> describes. After days of rain the sand loosens and grips the post less, and storms often bring days of rain with them. That is why no wood-fence post of ours goes in shallower than 24 inches, each one gets an 80-pound bag of concrete, and holes get deeper or wider where the sand is loose or the ground stays wet. Taller fences and gate and corner posts go deeper still.</p>

<h2>Fence styles ranked for a windy lot</h2>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Ornamental aluminum or steel.</strong> Almost no wind load. A limb strike bends one panel, which is replaced on its own. Both run $40&ndash;$100 a foot on our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Chain link without slats.</strong> Wind passes through. Add slats and it behaves more like a solid fence.</li>
  <li><strong>Split rail and ranch wire.</strong> Good for marking acreage lines north of Willis or around Huntsville, with nothing to catch the wind. Brace assemblies at corners and gates do the real work.</li>
  <li><strong>Shadowbox and lattice-top wood or vinyl.</strong> The gaps relieve some pressure while keeping most of the privacy. See <a href="/fence-styles/semi-privacy/">semi-privacy fences</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Solid privacy (board-on-board, solid vinyl, composite).</strong> The most load. These work fine when the posts are built for it.</li>
</ol>
<p>On wood privacy, the frame matters as much as the posts. Ours get three rails fastened with Simpson brackets instead of toe-nails, and hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails that keep pickets on when the fence flexes.</p>

<h2>Lake lots and wooded lots</h2>
<p>On the east shore of Lake Conroe, lots in Seven Coves, Lake Conroe Hills, and Point Aquarius catch wind that has crossed open water with nothing to slow it. The same is true of waterfront lots on Lake Livingston at Island View Ranch Estates and The Shores. Open aluminum keeps the view and sheds that wind. Where owners want privacy between houses, we keep solid sections short and use stronger posts at the ends.</p>
<p>On wooded lots, the bigger threat is usually what falls on the fence, not wind pushing it over. No fence stops a falling pine. What helps is a build that fails one section at a time and costs little to fix, plus trimming dead limbs over the line before storm season. Our <a href="/faq/hurricane-season-fence-prep/">hurricane season checklist</a> covers that.</p>
<p>For storms that do bring a fence down, our <a href="/maintenance-repair/">repair team</a> can rebuild sections, and the guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-conroe-soil/">setting posts in Conroe soil</a> explains how we build for the ground under your fence.</p>
