<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Most failed posts we pull around Conroe were set too shallow for loose sand, sat in water trapped on the clay below, or rotted at the ground line. Roots and concrete finished badly at the top account for most of the rest. A storm usually just exposes a post that was already weak.</p>
</div>

<h2>What's under the post</h2>
<p>NRCS soil survey data puts Conroe loamy fine sand on more of Montgomery County than any other soil. The USDA's <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">official description</a> gives it a gravelly sandy surface only 3 to 8 inches thick in the typical profile, then clay that drains slowly, with plinthite starting 23 to 43 inches down. Put a standard post hole in that profile and the top grips poorly, the middle holds water, and the bottom may be ironstone.</p>
<p>Other common soils are wetter. <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/S/SPLENDORA.html" rel="noopener">Splendora fine sandy loam</a> carries a perched water table 15 to 28 inches down from December to April in normal years, and <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/F/FETZER.html" rel="noopener">Fetzer loamy fine sand</a> is somewhat poorly drained, with water perching near the surface for short spells in winter and spring. You can look up your own lot on the USDA <a href="https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov/" rel="noopener">Web Soil Survey</a>.</p>

<h2>The five causes we see most</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>A shallow set in loose sand.</strong> Sand digs easily and grips poorly, so a post set at bare-minimum depth works loose in the first hard wind. Tall solid fences and gate posts go first, and a heavy drive gate on acreage puts more load on its post than anything else on the line.</li>
<li><strong>Water trapped in the hole.</strong> Rain soaks through the sand and stops at the clay, so a hole can hold water for days. On the wetter soils above, a winter hole can reach the perched water itself. Concrete poured into that water cures weak, and a wood post standing in it stays wet.</li>
<li><strong>Rot at the ground line.</strong> Wood posts rarely rot at the top or deep underground. They rot in the few inches at grade, where air, water, mulch, and sprinkler spray meet. The post looks fine until it snaps in a storm.</li>
<li><strong>Concrete finished the wrong way.</strong> Concrete left below grade or dished at the top forms a cup that holds water against the wood. A thin collar of concrete around a post, instead of a full footing, also breaks loose easily.</li>
<li><strong>Roots and trees.</strong> On wooded lots, growing roots shove posts out of line, and leaning trees or heavy limbs load the fence from above. Pines and oaks right on the line are a frequent cause of lean on east-side and lake lots.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What prevents each one</h2>
<p>Depth, water, and the top of the concrete are all decided on install day. Our wood-fence posts get a 24-inch minimum and 80 lb of concrete apiece, more in loose or soggy ground, more for taller fences, and more again at gates and corners depending on gate size and soil. When a hole reaches ironstone, we keep going. We deal with water in the hole before the pour, shape the concrete to shed water, and add a 6-inch rot board so the pickets aren't sitting in dirt. Galvanized steel posts behind <a href="/materials/wood-cedar/">wood pickets</a> are an option where ground-line rot is the main risk. Our article on <a href="/resources/fence-posts-conroe-soil/">how we set posts in Conroe soil</a> explains the full method, and costs for each material are on our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>.</p>
<p>After the build, keep sprinklers aimed away from the fence, pull mulch and pine straw back from the base, and trim limbs that rub against the boards or hang over the line.</p>

<h2>One bad post or the whole line?</h2>
<p>One leaning post beside a gate or under a tree is usually a repair. When several posts lean the same way, or a post moves at the base when you push it, the rest of the line may be failing at the same rate. Where it broke tells you something too: a clean snap at grade is rot, while a post that tips with its concrete still attached was set too shallow for the sand. The <a href="/resources/fix-leaning-fence-post-conroe/">leaning post repair guide</a> shows how to tell the difference.</p>
