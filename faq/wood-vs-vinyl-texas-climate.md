<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Around Conroe, the choice comes down to how much shade, sun, and falling debris your lot gets. Wood costs less and fixes board by board, but our wet, humid weather means it needs sealing every few years. Vinyl never needs stain and won't rot, yet it costs more and a heavy pine limb can crack a rail.</p>
</div>

<h2>Our rain, by the numbers</h2>
<p>NOAA's 1991&ndash;2020 normals for the Conroe station come to <a href="https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/us-climate-normals/#dataset=normals-monthly&amp;timeframe=30&amp;station=USC00411956" rel="noopener">about 51 inches of rain a year</a>. It doesn't arrive evenly. May, June, and October are the wettest months, and each averages more than 5 inches. July is drier, with highs near 95.</p>
<p>That pattern is hard on wood. A board soaks through in a wet spring, then bakes in July, and the swing is what makes it twist and split over time. The bottom few inches stay wet longest, which is why every wood fence we build gets a 6-inch rot board, and why we suggest sealing in the first few months.</p>
<p>Vinyl doesn't absorb water, so the rain mostly leaves it alone. In humid shade it grows mildew on the side that sees the least sun. That's a cleaning chore, not damage.</p>
<p>Underneath both, the USDA describes the common Conroe soil as <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">loamy fine sand over slow-draining clay</a>. After a heavy rain, water sits where the sand meets the clay, right around the base of a post. A wood post rots at that line over the years. A vinyl post won't rot, but either one leans if the hole was shallow. Every post we set goes in concrete, and wood posts go at least 24 inches deep with 80 pounds of concrete.</p>

<h2>Termites change the math in some areas</h2>
<p>Native subterranean termites live throughout our service area and go after the same damp bottom boards that rot does. Texas A&amp;M AgriLife's urban entomology program lists Liberty and Polk among the Texas counties where the more aggressive <a href="https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/termites/formosan/" rel="noopener">Formosan termite</a> has been detected. That takes in Cleveland and part of the Lake Livingston shoreline. If you're out that way and set on wood, galvanized steel posts behind the pickets remove the part termites hit hardest. Our <a href="/resources/termite-damage-wood-fence/">termite guide</a> covers the rest.</p>

<h2>Match the material to the lot</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Under pines and hardwoods.</strong> Harper's Preserve, Graystone Hills, and Lake Chateau Woods have plenty of shade. Wood stays damp longer there, and pine straw piles against the bottom rail. Vinyl holds up structurally, but limbs are the bigger risk. A cracked vinyl rail means ordering a matching part, while a broken cedar picket is a quick swap from stock lumber.</li>
  <li><strong>Open lots in new sections.</strong> Bare wood on a south or west run fades to gray within a season or two. Vinyl handles the sun well if it's a UV-stabilized product installed with room to expand. See <a href="/faq/will-vinyl-warp-in-heat/">will vinyl warp</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Lake lots.</strong> On Lake Conroe's east shore, wind off the water and constant moisture favor vinyl or open metal over wood on the lake side. Whatever the material, it has to stay off SJRA land below the 201-foot contour.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The HOA may decide it for you</h2>
<p>Check the rules before you price either one. Point Aquarius sends every fence to its Architectural Control Committee, and Seven Coves has deed restrictions that differ by section. In Evergreen, vinyl suits owners in Trilogy who'd rather not maintain a fence, if the association approves it. Lake Chateau Woods has no HOA, so the choice is yours. We check the current guidelines for your address as part of every quote. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page explains how that review works.</p>

<h2>Cost and upkeep over time</h2>
<p>On our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>, treated pine runs about $25&ndash;$35 per installed foot and cedar $30&ndash;$47. Vinyl runs $25&ndash;$70, with privacy panels starting around $50. Thicker, higher-quality vinyl sits at the upper end, and it resists sagging and cracking in heat better than thin panels. Wood needs a fresh coat of sealer every few years, which narrows the gap over time. If you plan to sell in a few years, wood usually makes more sense. If you're staying ten years or more and would rather not stain, vinyl tends to pay off. Compare the details on our <a href="/materials/wood-cedar/">wood and cedar</a> and <a href="/materials/vinyl/">vinyl</a> pages.</p>
