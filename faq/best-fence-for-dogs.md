<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> For most dogs around Conroe, a 6-foot solid wood or vinyl fence with a dig barrier along the base and self-latching gates does the job. Small dogs need tight spacing at the bottom, jumpers need height with nothing to climb on, and diggers need protection below grade. On lots where the HOA requires an open fence, a mesh liner or close picket spacing fills the gaps.</p>

<h2>Start with how your dog gets out</h2>
<p>At the estimate, the first questions are about the dog, not the fence: size, age, and how it has gotten out before. Each escape route has its own fix.</p>

<h2>Sandy ground and diggers</h2>
<p>The most common soil in Montgomery County is Conroe loamy fine sand. The <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">USDA soil description</a> puts a sandy surface over slow-draining clay, and the sand digs fast. A determined dog can tunnel under a fence in an afternoon.</p>
<p>Rain makes it worse. NOAA's 1991&ndash;2020 normals for the <a href="https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/us-climate-normals/#dataset=normals-monthly&amp;timeframe=30&amp;station=USC00411956" rel="noopener">Conroe weather station</a> show May, June, and October each averaging more than 5 inches. Water running along a fence line washes sand out from under the bottom board, and that small gap is where many escapes start. Pine roots can lift the ground and open gaps too.</p>
<p>For diggers, we use a buried wire apron or a barrier set below grade along the base, and we close gaps under the bottom board when we build. Every wood fence we build also gets a 6-inch rot board along the bottom, which gives the barrier something solid to meet. After a heavy rain, walk the line and fill any new washouts. Before a new fence goes in, we request Texas 811 locates.</p>

<h2>When the HOA wants an open fence</h2>
<p>Lots that back to lakes, ponds, or greenbelts in communities like Grand Central Park, Harper's Preserve, Artavia, and Mavera often have rear-fence rules that favor open styles. Open ornamental fencing keeps the view but can let a small dog through. The fix is usually a mesh liner or puppy pickets near the ground, if your guidelines allow it.</p>
<p>Panorama Village is one place where the rule is written down. Its <a href="https://www.panoramavillagetx.gov/resident_services/planning_and_zoning.php" rel="noopener">fence permit packet</a> says golf-course lots must use a 48-inch black wrought iron or aluminum fence, and it allows black mesh on the bottom 12 inches. For a small dog, that mesh matters. For a larger dog that can clear 48 inches, a fenced patio area closer to the house may work better. We check the current rules for your address before we design.</p>

<h2>Dogs on acreage</h2>
<p>On the one-acre lots in Deer Forest and Teaswood, the horse tracts at Red Oak Ranch, or the larger lots around Cleveland, fencing every foot in privacy wood rarely makes sense. Most owners fence a dog yard near the back door and put woven no-climb wire on the perimeter. We also build electric fence, which some acreage owners use alongside wire.</p>
<p>Livestock raise the stakes. Under <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.822.htm#822.013" rel="noopener">Health and Safety Code 822.013</a>, a person who sees a dog attacking livestock or fowl may kill it without liability, and the dog's owner is liable for the damage. If your dog can reach a neighbor's goats or horses, a tight dog yard is cheap insurance.</p>

<h2>Gates cause most escapes</h2>
<p>Most dogs that get out do it through a gate someone left open. Spring hinges that pull the gate shut, paired with a latch that catches on its own, fix that. Gate posts also take the most strain, so ours go deeper than line posts, with the depth set by the gate size and the soil. That keeps the gate from sagging and leaving a gap at the latch side.</p>
