<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> We don't know of reliable local data on how much a fence adds to a sale price, so we won't quote a percentage. What we can say is that buyers around Conroe expect a fenced backyard in most subdivisions, and a leaning or rotting fence becomes a repair item in negotiations. A fence that is in good condition, fits the street, and was approved by the HOA helps a home sell. Material matters less than condition.</p>
</div>

<h2>What buyers expect, by type of property</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Master-planned subdivisions.</strong> In Grand Central Park, Artavia, Mavera, and similar communities, buyers expect a sound privacy fence in the approved style and stain. A builder fence that has started to lean shows up in listing photos and inspections.</li>
  <li><strong>Established neighborhoods.</strong> In places like Graystone Hills and Silverstone, many fences date to the early 2000s. A recent replacement stands out, and one at the end of its life gets noticed.</li>
  <li><strong>Lake lots.</strong> On Lake Conroe, the view is often what the buyer is paying for. An open aluminum fence that keeps the water in sight usually makes more sense than a solid one, provided it sits on your side of the 201-foot contour where SJRA land begins.</li>
  <li><strong>Golf-course lots.</strong> Some communities set the style for you. In Panorama Village, the city's permit packet calls for 48-inch black wrought iron or aluminum on golf-course lots.</li>
  <li><strong>Acreage.</strong> On acre-plus lots like those in Teaswood, buyers look at the condition of long perimeter runs and the drive gate. In communities that allow horses, such as Texas Grand Ranch near Huntsville, and on rural land around Cleveland, buyers with animals also look for cross-fencing and gates wide enough for a trailer.</li>
  <li><strong>Lake Livingston weekend homes.</strong> Buyers there often want low upkeep, so vinyl or aluminum in good shape is easier to sell than wood that needs work.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Condition counts more than material</h2>
<p>A well-kept cedar fence will do more for a sale than a damaged vinyl one. Buyers and inspectors notice posts that lean, gates that drag, gray and split boards, rust streaks from bare nails, and sections patched in mismatched lumber. Each of those looks like deferred maintenance, and buyers tend to estimate the cost on the high side.</p>
<p>Approval matters too. In communities with architectural review, a fence built without approval, or in a color the HOA doesn't allow, can turn into a question during the sale. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page explains how we handle approvals.</p>

<h2>Before you list: repair, refinish, or replace</h2>
<ol>
  <li>Walk the line and push on each post. If most are firm, a repair is probably enough.</li>
  <li>Fix gates first. Buyers open them, and a dragging gate makes a bad first impression.</li>
  <li>Power wash and stain wood so it looks good in listing photos. See <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a>.</li>
  <li>Replace runs where posts are failing, in the style your HOA already approves.</li>
</ol>
