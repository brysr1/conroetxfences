<div class="lc-callout">
  <p><strong>Short answer:</strong> We don't know of reliable local data on how much a fence adds to a sale price, so we won't quote a percentage. What we can say is that buyers around Conroe expect a fenced backyard in most subdivisions, and a leaning or rotting fence becomes a repair item in negotiations. A fence that is in good condition, fits the street, and was approved by the HOA helps a home sell. Material matters less than condition.</p>
</div>

<h2>What buyers expect, by type of property</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Master-planned subdivisions.</strong> In Grand Central Park, Artavia, Mavera, and similar communities, buyers expect a sound privacy fence in the approved style and stain. On the 45- to 60-foot lots in Grand Central Park's final phase, the fence is a large share of what shows in a backyard photo, and a builder fence that has started to lean is hard to hide.</li>
  <li><strong>Established neighborhoods.</strong> In places like <a href="/neighborhoods/silverstone/">Silverstone</a>, where many homes went up in the mid-2000s, plenty of fences are original or close to it. A recent replacement stands out, and one at the end of its life gets noticed.</li>
  <li><strong>Lake lots.</strong> On Lake Conroe, the view is often what the buyer is paying for. An open <a href="/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/">aluminum or steel</a> fence that keeps the water in sight usually makes more sense than a solid one, provided it sits on your side of the 201-foot contour where <a href="https://www.sjra.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Lake-Conroe-Rules-and-Regulations-2015.pdf" rel="noopener">SJRA land begins</a>. Under SJRA's rules, a structure placed on Authority land without a permit can be ordered removed at the owner's expense, which is not something a buyer wants to inherit.</li>
  <li><strong>Golf-course lots.</strong> Some communities set the style for you. In Panorama Village, the city's permit packet calls for 48-inch black wrought iron or aluminum on golf-course lots.</li>
  <li><strong>Acreage.</strong> On roughly one-acre lots in <a href="/neighborhoods/teaswood/">Teaswood</a>, buyers look at the condition of long perimeter runs and the drive gate. In communities that allow horses, such as <a href="/neighborhoods/texas-grand-ranch/">Texas Grand Ranch</a> near Huntsville, and on rural land around Cleveland, buyers with animals also look for cross-fencing and gates wide enough for a trailer. See <a href="/acreage-ranch-fencing/">acreage and ranch fencing</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Lake Livingston weekend homes.</strong> Buyers there often want low upkeep, so vinyl or aluminum in good shape is easier to sell than wood that needs work.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Who actually judges the fence</h2>
<p>Texas home inspectors follow standards set by the Texas Real Estate Commission, and those <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/22-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-535-227" rel="noopener">standards of practice</a> say the inspector is not required to inspect fences. Some do anyway, but you can't count on the report to flag a problem or to clear one. The fence gets judged by the buyer's eyes, the listing photos, and the agent on the walk-through.</p>
<p>In an HOA community, the association can weigh in too. When a home sells, the association can be asked for a resale certificate, and Texas Property Code <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.207.htm#207.003" rel="noopener">section 207.003</a> requires it to describe conditions on the lot the board knows violate the restrictions. A fence built without approval, or stained a color the committee didn't allow, can show up there. In a community with no HOA, like <a href="/neighborhoods/lake-chateau-woods/">Lake Chateau Woods</a>, there's no approval to show, and condition is the whole story.</p>

<h2>Condition counts more than material</h2>
<p>A well-kept cedar fence will do more for a sale than a damaged vinyl one. Buyers notice posts that lean, gates that drag, gray and split boards, rust streaks from bare nails, and sections patched in mismatched lumber. Each of those looks like deferred maintenance, and buyers tend to estimate the cost on the high side. Pine straw piled at the base and green mildew on the shady side are common on wooded Conroe lots and are easy to fix before photos. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page explains how we handle approvals.</p>

<h2>Before you list: repair, refinish, or replace</h2>
<ol>
  <li>Walk the line and push on each post. If most are firm, a repair is probably enough.</li>
  <li>Fix gates first. Buyers open them, and a dragging gate makes a bad first impression.</li>
  <li>Power wash and stain wood so it looks good in listing photos. See <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a>.</li>
  <li>Replace runs where posts are failing, in the style your HOA already approves, and file the approval letter with your closing documents.</li>
  <li>If you share a fence with a neighbor, settle any repair of the shared run before listing. We can break out that section's cost on the quote so both owners see their share.</li>
</ol>
