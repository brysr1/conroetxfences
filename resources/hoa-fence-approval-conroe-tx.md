<p>In most of the newer communities around Conroe, the fence you want has to clear an architectural review before a single post goes in. The committee usually isn't trying to stop the fence. It wants enough detail to know the fence will match the street, stay off common areas and easements, and use an approved height, material, and color.</p>
<p>Most delays we see come from incomplete applications, not denials. Here is the order we work in when we help a homeowner get approval, and how the review changes from one kind of Conroe lot to another.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Get the current rules, not last year's copy</h2>
<p>Every community writes its own guidelines, and many amend them over time. Grand Central Park, Artavia, and Mavera are each run through a management company with a resident portal where the design guidelines are posted. Harper's Preserve has a community association that enforces its deed restrictions. Seven Coves posts its deed restrictions by section, so the rules on one street can differ from the next, and Point Aquarius has an Architectural Control Committee.</p>
<p>Download the guidelines and find three things: the fence section, the approved stain or color list, and the application form. If your community was built in phases, confirm which section your lot is in. We check this for every quote, but a homeowner who already has the document saves a week.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Find your lot lines and what touches them</h2>
<p>Pull the survey from your closing papers. It shows lot lines, easements, and building lines, and the committee will want your fence drawn on it or on the plat. Reviewers commonly look at:</p>
<ul>
<li>How far back from the front corner of the house the fence starts</li>
<li>Utility and drainage easements, which some communities keep clear or require to stay open</li>
<li>Rear lines that back to a lake, pond, trail, or preserve, where an open style is often required</li>
<li>Corner lots, where the side facing the street may have its own setback</li>
</ul>
<p>Can't find the survey? Our answer on <a href="/faq/property-survey/">when you need a property survey</a> explains the options.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Build a complete submittal</h2>
<p>A complete package usually covers the site sketch with the fence line and gates, height, material, style (side-by-side, board-on-board, shadowbox, or ornamental), post type, which side faces out, and the stain by product and color name. Some committees also want a photo of a similar fence or a product sheet for metal panels.</p>
<p>We prepare this from our own measurements, so the drawing and the quote describe the same fence. Many portals require the homeowner to submit under their own login, and we walk you through it.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Wait for the approval in writing</h2>
<p>Ask the management company how often the committee meets and how long reviews take, then plan the install around that. Spring brings the most applications and the busiest fence schedules. Never let anyone start on a verbal okay. Without the letter, an owner can be told to change or remove a finished fence at their own expense.</p>
<p>If the approval comes back with conditions, read them closely. Common ones are a deadline to stain the new fence in the approved color, or a requirement to match the height of an adjoining fence where you tie in.</p>

<h2>How the review changes by lot type</h2>
<h3>Greenbelt, pond, and preserve lots</h3>
<p>Harper's Preserve wraps around a 160-acre forested preserve, and Grand Central Park has miles of trails and several lakes, so both have many lots backing to open space. Mavera and Artavia have pond and lake lots with the same question. Rear fences on those lines get closer review and are more likely to be required in an open ornamental style, so we check before suggesting a privacy fence across the back.</p>
<h3>Panorama Village golf lots</h3>
<p>Panorama Village is a city rather than an HOA, but its review works much the same way. The city's fence packet requires a plot plan, Planning and Zoning Commission approval (a written decision within 30 days), a $100 permit fee, and an inspection when the fence is done. Lots that back to the course are limited to one look: black wrought iron or black aluminum, exactly 48 inches tall, with openings of 3 to 4 inches and a smooth rail along the top. Black mesh is permitted across the lowest foot. Chain link is allowed elsewhere but not facing the course. More on our <a href="/neighborhoods/panorama-village/">Panorama Village page</a>.</p>
<h3>Lake Conroe and Lake Livingston lots</h3>
<p>On a Lake Conroe waterfront lot, the association is only one approval. SJRA owns the shoreline strip up to the 201 ft contour, and building anything permanent on that strip takes an SJRA permit. On Lake Livingston, the Trinity River Authority handles permits near the water, and communities such as Westwood Shores have their own Architectural Control Committee. We confirm both before we draw the line.</p>

<h2>Step 5: Build exactly what was approved</h2>
<p>With approval in hand, we request Texas 811 locates and set the install date. The crew works from the approved drawing, not from memory, and the walkthrough checks the finished fence against it. Keep the approval letter with your house records. It is useful when you sell, and it settles questions if the committee's members change.</p>
