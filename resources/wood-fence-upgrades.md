<p>Most wood fence quotes look alike until you get to the extras. The base build covers pickets, rails, and posts, and the wood fence upgrades decide how long it all stays straight and how good it looks after a few Conroe summers. Some extras pay for themselves by heading off the repairs we see most often in Conroe, Willis, and Montgomery. Others are about looks, which is fine as long as you know which is which.</p>
<p>Here are five we'd put money into, roughly in the order we'd spend it.</p>

<h2>The wood fence upgrades that prevent repairs</h2>

<h3>1. Galvanized steel posts behind the pickets</h3>
<p>Wood fences around here usually fail at the ground line, where a wood post sits in damp soil and rots or gets eaten by termites. Galvanized steel posts don't rot there. Set behind the pickets, they're hidden from the yard side, and the fence still looks like a wood fence. If you only pay for one upgrade, this is usually the one, especially on shaded, wooded lots where the ground stays wet after every rain.</p>

<h3>2. A steel-frame gate with heavy hinges</h3>
<p>An all-wood gate wants to fold into a parallelogram under its own weight, and damp shade loosens every joint. A welded steel frame with wood pickets on the face stays square far longer, and heavy strap hinges spread the load instead of pulling screws out of soft wood. For a gate you open every day, or a wide one that swings across the driveway, this upgrade saves the most aggravation. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fix-sagging-fence-gate/">fixing a sagging fence gate</a> shows what goes wrong without it.</p>

<h3>3. A taller rot board</h3>
<p>Our wood fences come with a 6-inch rot board along the bottom as standard. It keeps the pickets out of wet grass, mulch, and pine straw, and when it wears out, you replace one board instead of a whole row of pickets. On lots where sprinklers hit the fence, beds are built up against it, or the dog digs along the line, a taller rot board buys more room.</p>

<h2>The upgrades for looks and lifespan</h2>

<h3>4. A top cap and trim board</h3>
<p>A cap runs along the top of the pickets and a trim board sits just under it. It gives the fence a heavier, finished look from the patio, and the cap also covers the end grain at the top of the pickets, where rain soaks in. Some communities around Conroe call for a specific cap or trim detail in their fence guidelines. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing page</a> covers how we check your community's current guidelines.</p>

<h3>5. Stain, planned from the start</h3>
<p>Sun on south and west runs fades wood quickly, and humidity keeps shaded runs damp enough for mildew. A penetrating stain slows both. We plan the color with the fence, then apply it once the wood is dry enough to take it, which can mean waiting a while on new pine in humid weather. Choosing it up front also means the HOA application shows the final color the first time.</p>

<h2>What can wait</h2>
<p>Not every extra earns its keep on day one. Decorative post caps and lattice tops are easy to add later. Board-on-board pickets cost more lumber than side-by-side, and whether they're worth it depends on how much the gaps from shrinking pickets would bother you. If the budget is tight, put the money underground and into the gate first. Pickets and trim are cheap to change later. Posts are not.</p>
