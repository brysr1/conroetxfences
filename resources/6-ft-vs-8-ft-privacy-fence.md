<p>The 6 ft vs. 8 ft privacy fence question comes up most on newer lots around Conroe, Willis, and Montgomery, where a two-story house next door or a busy road sits right behind the yard. Six feet is what most backyards here have and what most HOAs expect. Eight feet blocks more of the view and more of the headlights, but it costs more, catches more wind, and isn't allowed everywhere. Here is how the two compare, side by side.</p>

<h2>Privacy: what the extra two feet actually hides</h2>
<p>A 6-foot fence blocks the view of someone standing in the yard next door, which is most of what a backyard fence is for. Where it falls short is anywhere the neighbor stands higher than you: a raised patio, a deck a few steps up, or a lot that sits higher than yours. Eight feet closes most of that gap. It also does a better job against headlights and traffic on a road behind the house.</p>
<p>What neither height does is block a second-story window. If the neighbor's upstairs bedroom looks straight down into your pool, the fence helps at the edges of the yard, and trees or a pergola do the rest. Be honest with yourself about which view bothers you before paying for height.</p>

<h2>Cost and materials</h2>
<p>An 8-foot fence uses longer pickets and longer posts, more concrete in each hole, and usually more framing to keep the taller boards flat. On wood, that means 8-foot boards in place of 6-foot ones on every foot of the run. Expect the per-foot price to land above the 6-foot ranges on our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>. We price both heights on the same layout when you're undecided, so you can see the real difference for your lot.</p>
<p>Some materials limit you more than others. Wood and composite build to 8 feet without much trouble. Vinyl privacy panels come in set heights, so check what's available in the color you want before you settle on a design.</p>

<h2>Wind load, posts, and sandy ground</h2>
<p>This is where the extra height costs the most. A solid fence works like a sail, and the post acts as a lever against the soil at its base. Going from 6 to 8 feet adds a third more board area, and because that area sits higher, the leverage at ground level goes up by roughly 1.8 times. That is why taller runs need deeper posts or closer spacing.</p>
<p>The ground under most of Conroe, Willis, and Montgomery makes that worse. A typical lot has 18 to 30 inches or more of loose sandy loam over a clay and plinthite subsoil, and loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways. Gate and corner posts lean first, so they get deeper, wider piers belled at the base. On an 8-foot fence, the line posts get longer and deeper too. Every post goes in concrete, never gravel, and any water in the hole gets pumped or bailed out before the pour. Our 24-inch, 80 lb floor on wood fences is a starting point for 6 feet, not a spec for 8. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-conroe-soil/">setting fence posts in Conroe soil</a> walks through how we read the hole.</p>

<h2>Rules on fence height</h2>
<p>Check the rules before you fall for the taller fence. Many master-planned communities around Conroe set a maximum backyard fence height in their guidelines, and 6 feet is common. Cities have their own limits, and some lots near Conroe are inside a city while their neighbors are in unincorporated county. The International Residential Code, which many cities adopt, exempts fences up to 7 feet tall from a building permit, so an 8-foot fence can trigger a review a 6-foot one wouldn't. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page covers the committee side, and we confirm both for your address before quoting.</p>

<h2>The practical takeaway</h2>
<p>For most Conroe backyards, a well-built 6-foot fence is the right call. Choose 8 feet where something specific justifies it: a road behind the yard, a neighbor's raised deck, or a pool you want fully screened. Many owners get the best of both by going 8 feet on one problem side and 6 feet everywhere else. Whichever height you pick, put the money into the posts first.</p>
