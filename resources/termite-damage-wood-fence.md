<p>Termites go after the same part of a wood fence that rot does: the bottom few inches, where the wood stays damp and touches soil. That is why termite damage is so often found by accident, when a post snaps in a storm or a picket crumbles under a weed trimmer. By the time you can see it from the patio, the insects have usually been at it for a while.</p>
<p>We repair and replace fences. We do not treat termites. This guide covers how to recognize the damage, what we can fix, and how to keep the next fence from becoming a meal.</p>

<h2>Which termites live around here?</h2>
<p>Native subterranean termites are found across Texas, including every part of our service area. They nest in the soil and travel up into wood through mud tubes they build as they go.</p>
<p>The Formosan subterranean termite is a more aggressive species. The <a href="https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/termites/formosan/" rel="noopener">Formosan termite page</a> from Texas A&amp;M AgriLife&rsquo;s urban entomology group names Harris County, along with Liberty and Polk counties, among the places it is established, which takes in the <a href="/service-areas/cleveland-tx/">Cleveland area</a> and much of the Lake Livingston shoreline. Montgomery and Walker counties were not on that list at its last update, though Harris County is right next door. Formosan swarms happen at night in late May and early June, and the winged swarmers are drawn to lights. A cloud of them around a porch light that time of year is worth a call to a pest control company.</p>

<h2>Signs of termite damage on a fence</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mud tubes</strong> about the width of a pencil running up a post, a rot board, or the back face of a picket</li>
<li><strong>Boards that sound hollow</strong> or papery when you tap them with a screwdriver handle</li>
<li><strong>Tunnels along the grain</strong> when you break a damaged board open, often packed with soil</li>
<li><strong>A post that gives way at the soil line</strong> while the wood above it looks fine</li>
<li><strong>Stain or paint that bubbles</strong> over soft wood near the bottom of the fence</li>
</ul>
<p>Rot looks different. Rotted wood is soft and stringy, or cracked into small cubes, and usually wet, with no tubes or packed tunnels. Carpenter bees leave neat round holes about the size of a fingertip in rails and trim, with sawdust underneath. One fence can have all three problems at once.</p>

<h2>Where to look first</h2>
<p>Check the bottom foot of the fence in the spots that stay wettest: runs behind mulch beds, stretches a sprinkler hits every morning, low corners where water sits after rain, and the shaded side of wooded lots where pine straw piles up. Gate posts and rot boards deserve a close look too.</p>
<p>On lots that were cleared out of the woods, old stumps and buried roots near the fence line can feed a colony for years. That is common on newer streets carved out of the pines and on acreage around Huntsville and the lake.</p>

<h2>What can be repaired</h2>
<p>When the damage stops at a handful of pickets, the rot board, or one or two posts, we cut out the bad wood, replace it, and fix whatever was keeping that spot wet. If the colony is still active, have a licensed pest control company deal with it before the repair. Otherwise the new wood just becomes the next target.</p>
<p>If tubes and hollow wood turn up across several sections, or more than a few posts are soft at the base, a repair rarely holds. At that point we quote a full replacement alongside the repair, and you choose.</p>

<h2>Building a fence termites leave alone</h2>
<p>No wood fence is immune, but a few details make a large difference:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A rot board along the bottom.</strong> It keeps the pickets off the soil and is cheap to swap. We put one on every wood fence unless the owner opts out.</li>
<li><strong>Posts set in concrete.</strong> All our posts are set in concrete, so the bottom of the post isn&rsquo;t sitting in bare soil.</li>
<li><strong>Galvanized steel posts.</strong> Steel posts behind wood pickets remove the part of the fence that termites and rot attack most.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/materials/wood-cedar/">Cedar</a> instead of untreated pine.</strong> Cedar heartwood resists insects and rot better, though it is not immune.</li>
<li><strong>Clearance at the base.</strong> Keep mulch, soil, and pine straw below the bottom of the boards.</li>
</ul>
<p>To take wood out of the picture, <a href="/materials/composite-trex/">composite</a>, vinyl, and ornamental aluminum or steel give termites nothing to eat.</p>
