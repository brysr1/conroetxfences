<p>Goats are the animal that finds out how good your fence really is. A cattle fence that has held for years can leak goats in a week. If you are planning a goat fence on acreage around Conroe, Willis, or Montgomery, the plan has to account for how goats behave, not just how many acres you are closing in.</p>
<p>Here is what we look at before we quote one.</p>

<h2>Why goats are hard on a fence</h2>
<p>Goats test a fence in four ways, and a good design answers all of them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>They climb.</strong> Anything that gives a hoof a step, like a leaning brace, a horizontal rail on the inside, or a stump next to the line, becomes a ladder.</li>
<li><strong>They lean and rub.</strong> A goat scratching on the wire every day stretches it. Once it sags, they push through or climb over the low spot.</li>
<li><strong>They squeeze.</strong> Kids especially will slip under a gap at a swale or beside a gate that a calf would never try.</li>
<li><strong>They push their heads through.</strong> Horned goats reach through larger openings for grass on the other side, then can't pull back out because the horns catch.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Picking the wire for a goat fence</h2>
<p>Woven wire with small openings is the standard answer. No-climb mesh with narrow openings, or a goat-and-sheep style woven wire with small squares, keeps heads and hooves on your side. Field fence with large openings at the top can work for hornless adults, but it tends to catch horned goats and let small kids through, so we ask what you keep before we pick the spacing.</p>
<p>Barbed wire is a poor goat fence. Goats walk between the strands, and they get cut doing it. If your land already has a barbed line, it can often carry woven wire as long as the frame is sound.</p>
<p>For height, most goat keepers start at about 4 feet, and athletic breeds or bucks may call for more. Some owners also add a single electric strand on the inside, offset from the wire, to stop the leaning and rubbing. If you plan to, tell us at the estimate so the posts and braces suit it. Our <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm and ranch materials page</a> shows the wire types we install side by side.</p>

<h2>Corners, braces, and posts in sandy ground</h2>
<p>Woven wire is only as tight as its ends. H-braces and corner assemblies hold the tension for the whole run, and on a goat fence they work harder, because the animals lean on the wire every day.</p>
<p>The ground matters here. Most acreage in this zone around Conroe, Willis, and Montgomery has 18 to 30 inches or more of loose sandy loam over a clay and plinthite subsoil. Loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways, so corner, brace, and gate posts are the first to lean, and once a corner leans, every foot of wire behind it goes slack. We set those posts in deeper, wider concrete piers, belled at the base to lock into firmer ground. If a hole takes on water, we pump or bail it out before the pour. Our write-up on <a href="/resources/fence-posts-conroe-soil/">setting fence posts in Conroe soil</a> covers the method in more detail.</p>
<p>Along the bottom, we keep the wire tight to grade. Low spots, swales, and the dip at a creek crossing are where goats get out and where coyotes and loose dogs get in, so we walk the line and plan those spots before any wire goes up.</p>

<h2>Gates goats can't beat</h2>
<p>Gates are the usual weak point. Goats learn simple latches, push on a gate until the hinge side gives, and slip through the gap underneath or beside it. A few habits help:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Close the gaps.</strong> Hang the gate low and keep the side gap at the latch post narrow.</li>
<li><strong>Cover open tube gates.</strong> The bars on a standard ranch tube gate are far enough apart for a kid to walk through. Mesh fastened to the gate fixes that.</li>
<li><strong>Use a latch they can't nose open.</strong> A chain with a snap or a two-step latch outlasts a goat's patience.</li>
<li><strong>Set the gate posts like corners.</strong> A gate post that leans in sandy ground lets the gate drag, and a dragging gate gets propped open.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Trees, browse, and the fence line</h2>
<p>Goats browse more than they graze. Saplings, low limbs, and bark along the fence line get eaten, so plan the line with that in mind. Keep the fence off trunks rather than using a tree as a post, and expect anything within reach on either side of the wire to be pruned for you. On a wooded tract, a cleared strip along the line also makes it easier to spot a limb on the wire after a storm, before the goats find it.</p>

<h2>Check the rules before the goats arrive</h2>
<p>Some acreage subdivisions limit which animals you can keep, and inside city limits there may be animal rules of their own. Read your deed restrictions and check with the city or your property owners' association before you buy goats, then build the fence to match.</p>
