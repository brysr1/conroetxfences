<p>A Lake Conroe waterfront fence is a different job from a backyard fence in town. On the water side of a lot in Willis, Montgomery, or the Conroe side of the lake, the fence has to stop at the right line, keep the view you paid for, stand up in wet sandy ground, and end cleanly at a bulkhead or the shore. Most of the planning happens before anyone digs. Here is what we look at on a lake lot, in the order we look at it.</p>

<h2>Find the SJRA line first</h2>
<p>Around Lake Conroe, the land along the shoreline up to the 201-foot contour belongs to the San Jacinto River Authority, not to the homeowner. Plenty of owners mow that strip for years and assume it's theirs. SJRA's rules say no one may build or keep a private structure on the lake without a proper permit or license, and its <a href="https://www.sjra.net/lakeconroe/licensing-my-residential-structure/" rel="noopener">residential licensing page</a> lays out the paperwork and annual fees for structures that need one.</p>
<p>For a fence, the simplest plan is usually to keep it on your deeded lot. We pull the survey, find where your lot line sits relative to the shoreline, and lay out the fence so it ends on your side of that line. If the fence you want would cross onto SJRA land, that is a question for SJRA before we quote it, not after. Our <a href="/process/">process page</a> shows where this check falls alongside the HOA submittal and the Texas 811 request.</p>

<h2>Keep the view on the water side</h2>
<p>Most lake owners don't want a wall between the deck and the water. The usual answer is an open fence on the shoreline side and privacy only where it's needed, along the side yards near the house. Many lake communities around Willis and Montgomery have guidelines that push the same way, limiting solid fences near the water so neighbors keep their view down the shore.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ornamental aluminum</strong> is our most common pick on the water side. It doesn't rust, which matters with spray, humidity, and wet grass against the bottom rail.</li>
<li><strong>Ornamental steel</strong> is stronger and works where a gate takes hard use, but scratches in the coating need touch-up near the water.</li>
<li><strong>Wood or composite privacy</strong> fits the side yards, stepping down to metal partway toward the shore.</li>
</ul>
<p>Our <a href="/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/">ornamental steel and aluminum</a> page covers styles, heights, and colors.</p>

<h2>Posts in wet, sandy ground</h2>
<p>Lake lots in this part of Montgomery County sit on the same upland sand as the rest of Conroe and Willis: 18 to 30 inches or more of loose sandy loam over a clay and plinthite subsoil. Loose sand doesn't hold a post sideways, so gate and corner posts lean first. Near the shore, the bottom of the hole is often wet, and on a lot that slopes to the water, groundwater can seep in while we dig.</p>
<p>So every post goes in concrete, never gravel. Gate and corner posts get deeper, wider piers belled at the base. Any water in a hole gets pumped or bailed out before the pour. On wood fences, 24 inches and 80 lb of concrete per post is our floor, and lake lots often need more. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-conroe-soil/">setting fence posts in Conroe soil</a> explains how we read each hole.</p>
<p>Open designs also help when the lake comes up. SJRA raises releases from the dam after heavy rain, and a solid fence across a low spot catches water and debris like a dam. An open metal panel lets it pass.</p>

<h2>Where the fence meets a bulkhead</h2>
<p>On many lots the fence line doesn't end at a neighbor's fence. It ends at a bulkhead, a seawall, or the edge of a canal. We don't bolt the fence to the bulkhead or set a post where it could load the wall. Instead, the end post goes in its own pier, set back from the bulkhead, and the last panel is sized to close the gap. If your bulkhead is older or already leaning, have that looked at before the fence goes in, since a fence can't fix a failing wall.</p>

<h2>Gates, hardware, and kids near the water</h2>
<p>A dock gate gets used constantly in summer, often by someone carrying a cooler and a fishing rod. A self-closing hinge and a latch you can work with one hand make it pleasant to use. Near the water, choose stainless or heavy galvanized hinges, latches, and fasteners, since plain steel hardware rusts fast in lake humidity.</p>
<p>If young children or grandchildren visit, think about the gate as a barrier, not just a door. A latch mounted high on the gate and a self-closing hinge keep a toddler from wandering down to the dock. If there's a pool on the lot too, pool barrier rules apply to that fence, and we plan it to meet them.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Your survey,</strong> so the SJRA line and lot corners are clear.</li>
<li><strong>Your HOA or POA guidelines,</strong> especially anything on fences near the water.</li>
<li><strong>Where you walk to the dock,</strong> so the gate lands in the right place.</li>
<li><strong>Any bulkhead, seawall, or drainage problems</strong> you already know about.</li>
</ul>
